Priya Raghunathan, RN, MSN — career notes, written out for my own reference.

I started at St. Brigid Regional Hospital in Providence in June 2011, straight
out of my BSN at Rhode Island College. Med-surg floor, 32 beds, nights. I was a
staff nurse there until early 2015. The thing I'm proudest of from those years
is that I built the shift handoff sheet the floor still uses — before that,
handoff was verbal and we were losing things between shifts, mostly pending labs
and pain-management plans. I put together a one-page structured sheet with the
charge nurse and we went from about four handoff-related incident reports a
month to under one. I also precepted new grads, six of them over those years,
and two of them are charge nurses now. I got my ACLS in 2012 and have kept it
current ever since. We were on Meditech then, which I never loved.

In February 2015 I moved to Coastal Vascular Associates, an outpatient specialty
practice, as a clinical nurse. Much smaller — five providers, about 60 patients a
day. I was there until August 2018. What I actually did there was rebuild how
pre-op teaching worked. Patients were showing up for vascular procedures without
having stopped their anticoagulants, and we were cancelling maybe three cases a
week. I wrote the teaching materials, set up a call-back protocol two days out,
and got the cancellation rate down to under one a month within about six months.
I also ran the practice's transition to Epic in 2017 — I was the clinical lead
for it, worked with the vendor team, trained all fourteen clinical staff, and we
went live on schedule with no downtime beyond the planned weekend. That's where
I really learned Epic; I'd call myself expert on it now, seven-ish years.

Then I went back to the hospital system, but on the administrative side. County
Health Network, September 2018 to now. Started as a Nurse Care Coordinator and
moved to Charge Nurse over the ambulatory network in March 2021. Six clinic sites,
roughly 400 referrals a month coming through. The big piece of work was the
referral intake rebuild in 2022 — referrals were sitting an average of three
weeks before anyone triaged them, and some were falling off entirely. I mapped
the whole thing, found that everything was queuing at a single fax intake point,
and rebuilt it as a shared work queue with triage criteria the nurses could apply
themselves. Average wait went from three weeks to four days. We also stopped
losing referrals, which mattered more.

Since 2023 I've been running our quality improvement work for ambulatory. I lead
the PDSA cycles, sit on the network quality committee, and I've trained about
thirty staff on the improvement methodology. I did my MSN in Nursing Leadership
at UMass Boston, finished May 2020.

Other things: I've been bilingual English/Spanish my whole life and I use it
daily — probably a third of our patient population. I'm certified in Ambulatory
Care Nursing (ANCC, since 2019). I know my way around Tableau well enough to
build the quality dashboards we use, though I wouldn't call myself an analyst.
BLS obviously, current. I did a wound care certification in 2016 but I've let
that lapse and don't use it.

What I want next: I want to stay in ambulatory quality improvement, ideally
somewhere that takes health equity seriously — I've done a lot of work on
language-access gaps and I want that to be part of the job, not a side project.
I do not want to go back to inpatient nights. I don't want a role that's purely
data analysis with no clinical contact. And I've had enough of organizations
where quality improvement is a compliance exercise for accreditation — if the
posting is all about Joint Commission readiness I'm not interested.
